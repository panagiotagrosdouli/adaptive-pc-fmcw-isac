\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work examined adaptive PC-FMCW ISAC from a deliberately operational perspective: before asking which waveform configuration is preferable, the controller must first determine which configurations are physically capable of supporting the requested state. That separation is important in high-mobility operation, where range, sampling, and unambiguous-velocity limits can invalidate an otherwise attractive action before communication or sensing QoS is considered. The proposed framework therefore applies a deterministic physical-admissibility gate first and performs uncertainty-aware joint QoS selection only over the configurations that remain.

The experiments show that robustness changes the shape of the usable operating region rather than simply improving every operating point. In the frozen paired benchmark, B4 acts less often than the deterministic joint controller B3, but the states it accepts are substantially more reliable under the declared uncertainty model. B4 selects 12.23\% of cases and succeeds in all 1,468 selected cases, corresponding to a one-sided 95\% Wilson lower bound of 99.816\%. B3 selects 19.07\% of cases and achieves 84.83\% conditional joint QoS. Because B4 abstains more often, its unconditional joint-QoS probability is lower; the paired B4--B3 difference is $-0.03942$ with a 95\% bootstrap interval of $[-0.04292,-0.03600]$. This negative result is central to the paper: the robust controller should be interpreted as a conservative operating-region selector, not as a universally superior policy.

The ablation and mismatch studies help explain where that behavior comes from. Removing the physical gate allows configurations that cannot represent the requested range or velocity to enter the optimization, while removing state uncertainty or the joint constraint expands coverage at the cost of conditional reliability. Moderate mismatch in CFO, Doppler, and interference can still be tolerated on the evaluated banks, but sufficiently severe SNR and interference mismatch remain clear failure modes. The finite-draw study also shows that statistical confidence is not free: small sample budgets can make the declared reliability target unattainable, whereas larger budgets improve decision stability at increased computational cost.

These results leave several practical questions open. The current implementation is simulation-based and its timing measurements come from an unoptimized Python/Linux host, not an embedded automotive platform. Hardware-in-the-loop and RF validation, faster confidence tests, action pruning, and more realistic oscillator and channel models are natural next steps. Within the scope studied here, however, the main conclusion is clear: physics-aware robust adaptation can make the boundary between feasible, reliable, and unsupported PC-FMCW operation explicit. For high-mobility ISAC, that boundary is at least as important as the choice of waveform itself.
