\begin{abstract}
Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) systems that reuse a frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) waveform for both radar sensing and data transmission face a basic but often hidden constraint: a waveform configuration can be statistically attractive yet physically incapable of representing the requested range or radial velocity. This paper studies that configuration problem for a 77-GHz phase-coded FMCW (PC-FMCW) vehicular physical layer. We build a reproducible signal model that separates the one-way communication path from the two-way monostatic sensing echo, samples sensing at the dechirped intermediate frequency, and exposes waveform parameters that directly determine range and velocity capability. We then construct a finite, interpretable 54-action configuration space from two waveform profiles, three phase-code chip budgets, three transmit-power backoffs, and three repetition factors. A deterministic physics gate rejects actions whose range, positive-IF support, or unambiguous-velocity limits cannot cover the requested state before any stochastic reliability calculation is attempted. Source-grounded 77-GHz profile analysis shows a short-range parking profile with approximately 0.175-m range resolution but only 8.41-m/s monostatic unambiguous radial velocity, whereas the high-mobility capability profile extends the corresponding velocity support to approximately 48.67 m/s. Pilot simulation evidence further shows the transition from poor sensing recovery to sub-resolution errors as IF SNR increases, and validates the DBPSK communication reference against its analytical AWGN curve. These results establish a physics-first design principle for PC-FMCW ISAC: physical representability should be treated as a prerequisite to reliability optimization, not as an after-the-fact performance statistic. The study is simulation-based and does not claim hardware validation or a commercial implementation of the composite high-mobility profile.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) aims to share waveform, spectrum, hardware, and signal-processing resources between wireless communication and environmental sensing \cite{liu2022isac,wang2022isac}. Vehicular operation is a particularly demanding setting because communication and sensing are both exposed to rapid state variation, Doppler, interference, synchronization errors, and tight latency constraints. Recent ISAC surveys place waveform design, synchronization, resource management, standardization, and practical validation among the central physical-layer and systems questions \cite{liu2022isac,wang2024isac,vehicular2022isac}.

Phase-coded FMCW is an attractive member of the ISAC waveform family because an FMCW carrier preserves the delay--Doppler structure used by automotive radar while phase modulation provides a communication degree of freedom. Experimental PC-FMCW work has established the sensing-and-communication principle, while subsequent studies have investigated receiver structures, waveform smoothing, coherent MIMO operation, code-dependent sensing, and interference mitigation \cite{kumbul2021experimental,kumbul2022receiver,kumbul2022smoothed,kumbul2022codes,kumbul2023mimo,kumbul2023performance,kumbul2024interference}. The contribution of this paper is deliberately different. We do not introduce phase coding, a new modulation format, or a new range--Doppler estimator. Instead, we address the configuration-selection question that arises when a finite PC-FMCW action must serve a moving vehicular state.

The key observation is physical rather than statistical. For an FMCW profile, bandwidth determines range resolution, IF sampling and chirp slope constrain positive-IF range support, and chirp repetition interval determines monostatic unambiguous radial velocity. Consequently, a profile with excellent range resolution can still be unusable for a high-speed target. If such a configuration enters a subsequent reliability optimizer, failures caused by deterministic aliasing or support limits can be incorrectly interpreted as stochastic unreliability. The correct order is therefore to establish physical representability first and optimize reliability only inside the admissible region.

This paper develops that physics-first view for a reproducible 77-GHz PC-FMCW PHY. The study has four objectives: (i) define a physically consistent joint communication/sensing model; (ii) derive profile-level capability boundaries from waveform parameters; (iii) construct an interpretable finite action space for adaptation; and (iv) demonstrate, with source-grounded parameters and pilot simulations, why the physical gate is necessary before robust QoS qualification.

The paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a unified PC-FMCW signal model that explicitly distinguishes one-way communication propagation from two-way monostatic sensing;
    \item an analytical capability layer linking bandwidth, chirp timing, IF sampling, range resolution, positive-IF range support, velocity resolution, and unambiguous radial velocity;
    \item a 54-action finite PHY configuration space whose dimensions have direct physical interpretations;
    \item a deterministic physics gate that removes configurations outside their declared operating region before reliability evaluation;
    \item source-grounded profile and pilot-simulation evidence that exposes the short-range/high-mobility configuration trade-off without presenting simulation as hardware measurement.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
The broader ISAC literature has established the dual-function waveform perspective and identified the coupled sensing/communication trade-off as a central design problem \cite{liu2022isac,wang2022isac,wang2024isac}. Vehicular ISAC adds rapid channel and target dynamics and therefore makes waveform timing, Doppler, synchronization, and implementation constraints especially important \cite{vehicular2022isac}. A recent vehicular-ISAC survey further emphasizes robustness, high mobility, hardware/model non-idealities, and prototype validation as practical research directions \cite{ma2026vehicularisac}.

PC-FMCW provides a particularly relevant physical-layer test case. Kumbul et al. experimentally investigated PC-FMCW for sensing and communications \cite{kumbul2021experimental}, and later work analyzed performance, receiver structures, smoothed waveforms, coherent MIMO, sensing codes, and interference mitigation \cite{kumbul2022receiver,kumbul2022smoothed,kumbul2022codes,kumbul2023mimo,kumbul2023performance,kumbul2024interference}. These studies motivate the waveform family and receiver decomposition used here. However, they do not make profile-level physical representability the explicit first-stage decision variable in a finite vehicular adaptation problem.

The present work is also distinct from robust resource allocation and robust ISAC beamforming. Those approaches address uncertainty in channel state, target location, or optimization variables \cite{wu2021robustv2x,lyu2024dualrobust}. Such uncertainty treatment is complementary to, rather than a substitute for, deterministic waveform capability checks. Our central design rule is that an action must first be physically capable of representing the requested state. Statistical reliability is evaluated only after this gate.

\section{System and Signal Model}
\subsection{PC-FMCW Transmit Signal}
For chirp index $m$ and fast time $t$, the idealized transmitted signal is
\begin{equation}
 s_m(t)=\sqrt{P_m}\exp\left(j[\pi\mu t^2+\phi_m(t)]\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mu=B/T_c$ is the chirp slope, $B$ is the swept bandwidth, $T_c$ is the active chirp duration, $P_m$ is transmit power, and $\phi_m(t)$ is the phase-coded communication waveform. The model is intentionally parameterized so that the physical capability consequences of $B$, $T_c$, $T_r$, and $F_s$ remain explicit.

\subsection{Monostatic Sensing}
For target range $R$ and radial velocity $v$, the sensing echo has two-way delay and Doppler
\begin{equation}
 \tau_r=\frac{2R}{c},\qquad f_{D,r}=\frac{2vf_c}{c}.
\end{equation}
After dechirping, the receiver operates on the beat/IF signal rather than directly sampling the 77-GHz carrier. With the simulator sign convention,
\begin{equation}
 f_b=\mu\tau_r+f_{D,r}.
\end{equation}
The resulting complex IF matrix is processed across fast and slow time using windowed FFTs. Doppler is estimated and removed from the beat-frequency estimate before range recovery, reducing the standard range--Doppler coupling bias used in the simulator.

\subsection{One-Way Communication}
The remote communication path has one-way delay and Doppler
\begin{equation}
 \tau_c=\frac{R}{c},\qquad f_{D,c}=\frac{vf_c}{c}.
\end{equation}
The reference modem uses multi-chip DBPSK after FMCW dechirping. Under AWGN, the noncoherent DBPSK reference has
\begin{equation}
 P_b=\frac{1}{2}\exp(-E_b/N_0).
\end{equation}
The reference raw information rate for $N_{\rm chip}$ chips per chirp is proportional to $N_{\rm chip}/T_r$. Residual frequency error rotates the baseband sequence according to physical chip times and therefore becomes increasingly important at high mobility.

\section{Physical Capability Layer}
For each waveform profile, the simulator derives
\begin{align}
 \Delta R &= \frac{c}{2B},\\
 R_{\max}^{(+\mathrm{IF})} &= \frac{cF_s}{4\mu},\\
 v_{\max} &= \frac{\lambda}{4T_r},\\
 \Delta v &= \frac{\lambda}{2N_cT_r},
\end{align}
where $F_s$ is the IF sample rate, $N_c$ is the number of slow-time chirps, and $\lambda=c/f_c$. The first quantity is the range resolution; the second is the positive-IF range support under the receiver convention; the third is the monostatic unambiguous radial-velocity support; and the fourth is the nominal Doppler resolution.

The source-grounded short-range profile is a 77-GHz automated-parking reference with $B=\SI{858}{MHz}$, $T_c=\SI{25.6}{\micro s}$, $T_r=\SI{115.8}{\micro s}$, $F_s=\SI{10}{MSPS}$, 256 samples/chirp, and 64 chirps/frame \cite{tiParking}. It yields approximately $\Delta R=\SI{0.175}{m}$, $R_{\max}^{(+\mathrm{IF})}=\SI{22.36}{m}$, $\Delta v=\SI{0.263}{m/s}$, and $v_{\max}=\SI{8.41}{m/s}$.

The high-mobility capability profile uses a 77-GHz carrier, $B=\SI{1}{GHz}$, $T_c=T_r=\SI{20}{\micro s}$, $F_s=\SI{37.5}{MSPS}$, 750 samples/chirp, and 128 chirps/frame. Its derived quantities are approximately $\Delta R=\SI{0.150}{m}$, $R_{\max}^{(+\mathrm{IF})}=\SI{56.21}{m}$, $\Delta v=\SI{0.760}{m/s}$, and $v_{\max}=\SI{48.67}{m/s}$. This profile is explicitly a composite capability reference rather than a claimed commercial preset.

\section{Physics-Gated Finite Action Space}
The adaptive PHY action is
\begin{equation}
 a=(p,N_{\rm chip},b,N_{\rm rep}),
\end{equation}
where $p$ selects one of the two profiles, $N_{\rm chip}\in\{16,32,64\}$ is the chip budget, $b\in\{0,3,6\}$ dB is transmit-power backoff, and $N_{\rm rep}\in\{1,2,4\}$ is the repetition factor. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
 |\mathcal{A}|=2\times3\times3\times3=54.
\end{equation}
The action dimensions are deliberately interpretable. Profile choice controls waveform capability and sampling burden; chip count changes communication rate and chip duration; power backoff changes link and sensing margin; and repetition changes rate and error protection.

For an estimated operating state $\hat S=(\hat R,\hat v,\ldots)$, the deterministic gate retains
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{phys}}(\hat S)=\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\hat R\le R_{\max}^{(+\mathrm{IF})}(a),\ |\hat v|\le v_{\max}(a)\}.
\end{equation}
If $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{phys}}(\hat S)=\varnothing$, the state is declared physically infeasible for the declared action set. This statement is stronger and more informative than saying that a Monte-Carlo reliability estimate is low: no amount of statistical averaging can make an aliased or unsupported waveform physically represent the requested state.

\section{Pilot Validation}
The pilot evidence in the repository is explicitly labelled simulation evidence, not measurement. The parking profile was evaluated at a target $(R,v)=(\SI{10}{m},\SI{3}{m/s})$ over 50 trials per SNR point. At IF SNR $-25$ dB, the range RMSE was $\SI{0.0214}{m}$ and the velocity RMSE was $\SI{0.0351}{m/s}$; at $-20$ dB they were $\SI{0.0115}{m}$ and $\SI{0.0301}{m/s}$, respectively. At $-30$ dB the corresponding errors were $\SI{7.42}{m}$ and $\SI{5.13}{m/s}$, illustrating a sharp transition in the noisy pilot regime. The repository reports that all trials at $-25$ dB and above were within one derived range and velocity resolution.

For the high-mobility capability profile, the pilot target is $R=\SI{20.28}{m}$ and $v=\SI{19.856}{m/s}$. This velocity was derived from a published 77-GHz one-way Doppler benchmark and is used here only as a stress-test state. At IF SNR $-30$ dB, the range RMSE was $\SI{0.0106}{m}$ and velocity RMSE $\SI{0.0946}{m/s}$; at $-25$ dB they were approximately $0.000165$ m and $0.0936$ m/s.

The communication reference uses 32 chips/chirp and was evaluated over 300,000 bits per $E_b/N_0$ point. The simulated BER closely tracks the analytical DBPSK expression: at 0 dB the simulation is 0.18363 versus 0.18394 theory, at 4 dB it is 0.04106 versus 0.04056, and at 8 dB it is 0.00092 versus 0.00091. This agreement validates the reference modem under its declared AWGN assumptions; it does not establish a fielded vehicular communication link.

\section{Discussion}
The profile calculations expose the main design issue directly. The parking profile offers finer velocity resolution than the high-mobility capability profile but its unambiguous velocity support is only about 8.41 m/s. A high-speed operating state around 19.86 m/s therefore cannot be made valid by increasing SNR or repeating Monte-Carlo trials; the profile itself is outside its declared velocity region. Conversely, the high-mobility profile expands velocity support to approximately 48.67 m/s while using a shorter repetition interval and a larger IF sampling burden.

This motivates a separation of responsibilities. The physical layer should first answer whether a configuration can represent the state at all. Only then should communication BER, sensing RMSE, interference, synchronization residuals, or probabilistic QoS be used to decide among the admissible actions. The later robust-selection study in this repository builds on this gate but is intentionally treated as a separate paper: Paper 1 establishes the physical configuration problem, whereas the reliability-qualification paper studies finite-sample robust selection and its reliability--availability--computation trade-off.

The communication pilot gives a complementary warning. At $E_b/N_0=8$ dB, the reference BER is about $9\times10^{-4}$ with zero residual frequency error, but rises to about 0.032 at a 5.1-kHz residual-frequency scale. This does not imply that high mobility inevitably produces that error; rather, it demonstrates that synchronization and Doppler compensation must be explicitly represented when a high-mobility PC-FMCW configuration is evaluated.

\section{Limitations and Claim Boundaries}
This work is a PHY-level simulation study. The parking profile parameters are grounded in published Texas Instruments reference-design documentation, while the high-mobility profile is a composite capability reference and is not presented as a commercial preset. SNR, $E_b/N_0$, residual frequency error, interference, and phase-noise variables are controlled simulation quantities. The pilot results are not RF measurements. The paper also does not claim trajectory prediction, ego-motion planning, packet scheduling, beam management, standards compliance, or embedded-ECU timing. The resource dimensions of the finite action space are interpretable control indices; they are not a measured energy model.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented a physics-first configuration framework for 77-GHz PC-FMCW ISAC in high-mobility vehicular links. The central result is structural: profile capability must be checked before stochastic QoS qualification. Source-grounded calculations show that a short-range parking profile can provide approximately 0.175-m range resolution while supporting only about 8.41 m/s monostatic unambiguous radial velocity, whereas a composite high-mobility capability profile extends that support to approximately 48.67 m/s. Pilot simulations validate the sensing and DBPSK reference paths under their declared assumptions and demonstrate why synchronization residuals become a material communication impairment at high Doppler scales. The resulting 54-action space provides an interpretable foundation for subsequent reliability-constrained adaptation, while preserving a strict distinction between deterministic physical infeasibility and stochastic performance failure.
